Egypt After the Pharaohs

A new ruling world arrives.

After centuries of native Egyptian dynasties, Alexander the Great conquered Egypt in 332 BCE. He was welcomed by Egyptian priests and recognised in Egyptian tradition as a legitimate ruler. After Alexander's death, his general Ptolemy I Soter established the Ptolemaic dynasty, which ruled Egypt for nearly three centuries.

A Greek Dynasty in Egypt

Two traditions, one throne.

The Ptolemaic rulers were of Macedonian Greek origin, but they adopted many Egyptian royal traditions. They presented themselves as pharaohs, supported Egyptian temples and participated in traditional religious practices.

At the same time, Greek language and culture became increasingly important, especially in administration and urban life.

Two cultural traditions existed side by side — and often blended together.
Ptolemaic rulers built temples in the traditional Egyptian style
Alexandria — Egypt's Mediterranean Gateway

A city built for the world.

Founded by Alexander the Great, Alexandria became one of the most important cities of the ancient Mediterranean — famous for scholarship, science, philosophy, literature, trade and architecture.

Scholarship Science Philosophy Literature Trade Architecture

Alexandria drew scholars, texts and ideas from across the ancient world — a genuine meeting point of cultures on Egyptian soil.

Knowledge travelled across cultures through Alexandria.

The Great Library

The Library of Alexandria became a legendary center of learning, gathering texts and scholars from across the ancient Mediterranean world.

The Pharos

The Lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the ancient world's famous monumental structures, stood near the city's harbor guiding ships safely to shore.

Featured Ruler

Cleopatra VII.

Last Active Ruler of the Ptolemaic Kingdom

Cleopatra VII

Cleopatra VII became one of the most famous rulers in Egyptian history. She was politically skilled, multilingual and deeply connected to Egyptian royal and religious traditions.

Her reign was shaped by the growing power of Rome and her political relationships with Julius Caesar and Mark Antony. After her death in 30 BCE, Egypt became a Roman province.

Queen Diplomat Political Strategist Egyptian Ruler
📌 Avoid presenting Cleopatra only as a romantic figure — she was a skilled diplomat and political strategist navigating one of the most turbulent periods in the ancient Mediterranean.
Egyptian Gods in a Changing World

Old gods, new combinations.

Egyptian religion continued during the Ptolemaic period — but new religious combinations appeared alongside long-established traditions.

☥

Serapis

The Ptolemies promoted the cult of Serapis, a deity whose identity combined Greek and Egyptian religious traditions.

𓁛

Isis

The worship of Isis became increasingly widespread and eventually reached many parts of the Mediterranean world.

𓅃

Horus

Traditional Egyptian gods continued to be worshipped in temples throughout Egypt, largely unchanged in practice.

The gods changed with the culture, but the religious tradition remained deeply rooted in Egypt.

Temples of the Ptolemaic Period

Egyptian temple-building continues.

Some of Egypt's best-preserved temples were built or completed during the Ptolemaic period, sustaining traditional worship for centuries to come.

Edfu Temple

Dedicated to Horus, the temple at Edfu is one of the best-preserved ancient Egyptian temples.

Dendera Temple

Associated with Hathor, the temple complex contains beautifully preserved reliefs and inscriptions.

Philae Temple

Dedicated especially to Isis, Philae became an important center of her worship across the Mediterranean.

Kom Ombo

A distinctive double temple dedicated jointly to Sobek and Horus.

Egypt Becomes Roman — 30 BCE

From kingdom to province.

After the defeat of Cleopatra VII and Mark Antony, Egypt was incorporated into the Roman Empire. Unlike many other provinces, Egypt was controlled directly by the Roman emperor through a governor — a reflection of just how valuable its agricultural wealth, especially grain production, was to Rome.

Roman Power, Egyptian Tradition

Roman emperors were sometimes represented in Egyptian temples wearing traditional pharaonic imagery — ruling as Roman emperors abroad, and as pharaohs on temple walls.

Roman power expressed through Egyptian tradition.

Faces of Roman Egypt

The Fayum Portraits.

The Fayum mummy portraits are among the most remarkable surviving artworks from Roman Egypt. They combine Egyptian funerary traditions with a naturalistic portrait style associated with the Greco-Roman world — faces that still meet our gaze after two thousand years.

A Tradition Transforms

The decline of the old religion — and the rise of a new one.

Eventually, Egypt underwent another major transformation, as Christianity spread and traditional temple religion gradually declined.

The Old Tradition Fades

Christianity spread through Egypt during the Roman period. Traditional temple religions gradually declined as Christian communities grew.

394 CEThe last known dated hieroglyphic inscription was carved at Philae — more than three thousand years after hieroglyphic writing first emerged.

Christianity & Coptic Egypt

Egypt became one of the important centers of early Christianity. The Egyptian language continued in a new written form known as Coptic, written primarily using the Greek alphabet with additional signs.

Coptic eventually became an important part of Egyptian Christian culture, carrying the Egyptian language into a new era.

A monumental tradition thousands of years old was approaching its end — but the language it carried lived on.

A Civilization Transforms

The End of an Ancient World.

The end of the Ptolemaic and Roman periods did not mean that Egyptian culture disappeared. Instead, Egypt entered another transformation. Ancient religious traditions gradually gave way to Christianity, while the Egyptian language continued through Coptic culture.

The temples became silent, but the stories, monuments and objects survived.
